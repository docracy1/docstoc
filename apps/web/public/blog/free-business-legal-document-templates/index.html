If you've ever searched "free contract template" or "free NDA template" at 11pm the night before you needed to send one, you already know the problem: most "free" template sites gate the actual document behind an email signup, a watermark, or a "premium" upsell the moment you try to download. The template itself — the thing you actually needed — is the bait, not the product.

docstoc started as exactly that: a free document library, no catch. That's still true today. Templates are free, no signup required, no watermark, no upgrade wall to get the real document instead of a preview.

What's actually in the free template library

The library covers the documents small businesses and freelancers reach for most often:

  • Contracts and agreements — independent contractor agreements, non-disclosure and non-circumvention agreements, commercial lease agreements
  • Employment documents — offer letters, at-will employment terms
  • Collections and demand letters — including a demand letter for an unpaid invoice, for when a polite reminder hasn't worked
  • General business documents — the kind of one-off paperwork every small business eventually needs and never has ready

Each template is a real, usable starting point — not a locked preview. You open it, edit it for your situation, and use it. If you also happen to need to chase down a client who hasn't paid the invoice tied to one of these contracts, that's the other half of docstoc — but the templates themselves don't require it.

Why "free" templates are usually not actually free

Worth naming the pattern directly, since it's so common: a site ranks for "free operating agreement template," you click through, and the actual template requires an account, a credit card on file for a "free trial," or a locked PDF that unlocks only after you refer three friends. None of that is free — it's a lead-gen funnel wearing a free label.

The honest version of "free template" means: you can get the document today, without giving up an email address, without a trial that auto-bills you next month, and without a watermark stamped across the page you're supposed to send to a client or landlord.

How to actually use a legal template well

A template is a starting point, not a finished contract. A few practical rules that make the difference between "this held up" and "this caused a fight":

Fill in every bracket, not just the obvious ones

Templates usually mark the fields you need to fill with brackets or placeholder text. It's easy to fill in names and dates and miss a smaller field — a notice period, a governing-law state, a payment due date — buried mid-document. Read the whole thing once before sending it, specifically hunting for anything still in placeholder form.

Match the template to the actual relationship

An independent contractor agreement and an employment offer letter are not interchangeable, and using the wrong one has real consequences — misclassifying a contractor as an employee (or vice versa) can create tax and labor-law problems that have nothing to do with how the underlying work relationship actually functions. Pick the template that matches reality, not the one that's easiest to find.

Don't skip the boring clauses

Termination terms, dispute resolution, and confidentiality clauses feel like filler until the day they're the only thing standing between you and a bad outcome. If a template includes them, keep them; don't strip a contract down to just the payment terms because that's the part that feels relevant right now.

Get a second read on anything with real money or liability attached

Templates are a great starting point for routine, low-stakes agreements. For anything with significant dollar amounts, IP ownership, or liability exposure, a template plus five minutes of a lawyer's time is cheaper than a dispute later. This is true regardless of which library the template came from.

Where templates fit with the rest of your paperwork

A signed contract is rarely the end of the workflow — it's usually the start of one. The agreement sets payment terms; eventually there's an invoice against those terms; sometimes that invoice runs late. docstoc's other tools cover the rest of that chain without switching products: generate the actual invoice with line items and tax, certify a signed document's exact contents with a tamper-evident hash if you ever need to prove it wasn't altered later, and — if payment goes overdue — draft a follow-up email with the right tone for how late it is.

None of that is required to use the templates. But it's there if the paperwork you just downloaded turns into a "did they actually pay" problem three weeks from now.

FAQ

Do I need to create an account to download a template?

No. Templates are available without signing up.

Are these templates legally binding once I fill them in and both sides sign?

A properly completed and signed template can be a legally binding contract, same as any other written agreement — but "legally binding" and "enforceable exactly the way you hoped" aren't the same thing. This isn't legal advice; for anything high-stakes, have a lawyer review the filled-in version before it's signed.

Can I edit the templates in Word or Google Docs?

Yes — templates are provided as editable documents, not locked PDFs, specifically so you can adapt the language to your situation.

What if I need a document type that isn't in the library?

The library keeps growing. In the meantime, a close template edited carefully is usually a better starting point than starting from a blank page.

Is there a catch — ads, upsells, watermarks?

No watermark, no forced upgrade to get the actual file. docstoc's paid plans exist for the invoice-chasing and business-automation features, not for gating the free templates.

How is this different from Docstoc.com, the old site?

If you remember the original Docstoc as a free document library — that's the same idea, rebuilt. The free templates are still free; the site now also handles invoicing, document certificates, and SSL automation for people who want one place instead of five separate tools.
