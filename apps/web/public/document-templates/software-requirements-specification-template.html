Software Requirements Specification: RouteLedger Driver App

Version: 1.3 · Date: March 9, 2026 · Author: Priya Raman, Product Manager, Calloway Plumbing Supply

1. Purpose

RouteLedger replaces the paper delivery sheets used by Calloway Plumbing Supply's 38 drivers across four branches in Ohio. Drivers confirm each delivery on a phone with a photo and the customer's signature; dispatchers see every truck's progress live and answer "where is my order?" calls without phoning the driver.

2. Scope

In scope: driver route list, proof of delivery (photo, signature, recipient name), partial deliveries and returns, live dispatcher map, automatic delivery confirmation email to the customer, daily export of completed deliveries to the ERP.
Out of scope: route optimization, customer self-service tracking portal, invoicing and payment collection on site. These are planned for version 2.

3. Functional Requirements

IDRequirementPriority
FR-1The system shall show each driver the day's stops in the order set by dispatch, with address, contact and order lines.Must
FR-2The system shall record proof of delivery: at least one photo, the recipient's printed name and an on-screen signature.Must
FR-3The system shall let a driver mark individual order lines as delivered, short or refused, with a reason code.Must
FR-4The system shall store deliveries on the phone when there is no signal and upload them within 60 seconds of reconnecting.Must
FR-5The system shall show dispatchers every active truck on a map, refreshed at least every 2 minutes.Must
FR-6The system shall email the customer a delivery confirmation with photo and signature within 5 minutes of completion.Should
FR-7The system shall export all completed deliveries to the ERP as a CSV file at 11:00 p.m. each day.Should
FR-8The system shall let a branch manager reassign a stop to another driver during the day.Could

4. Non-Functional Requirements

  • Performance: the route list opens in under 2 seconds on a 4G connection; a proof-of-delivery upload completes in under 10 seconds.
  • Availability: 99.5% during delivery hours (6:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. Eastern, Monday to Saturday).
  • Security: single sign-on with the company's existing Microsoft accounts; all data encrypted in transit and at rest; photos and signatures kept 7 years, then deleted.
  • Devices: Android 12 or later and iOS 16 or later; usable with work gloves (touch targets at least 48 px).
  • Scalability: up to 120 drivers and 1,500 stops a day without changes to the architecture.

5. User Roles

  • Driver: sees own route only; records deliveries, returns and notes.
  • Dispatcher: builds routes, sees all trucks of the branch, edits stop order.
  • Branch manager: everything a dispatcher can do, plus reassigning stops and viewing reports for the branch.
  • Administrator (IT): manages users, reason codes and the ERP export settings.

6. Constraints

Fixed budget of $180,000 for version 1, including the first year of hosting. Go-live at all four branches by September 1, 2026, before the fall heating season. The ERP can only receive data as a nightly CSV import; no real-time interface is available.

7. Assumptions and Dependencies

Every driver has a company phone with a data plan (purchase approved February 2026). Customer email addresses in the ERP are at least 90% complete; the sales team cleans up the rest by July 1, 2026. The map relies on a commercial mapping service whose license is already part of the dispatch budget.

What goes into an SRS

A software requirements specification describes what a system must do and how well, before anyone builds it. It is the shared reference for the client, the developers and the testers. The template follows the usual structure: purpose, scope, functional requirements, non-functional requirements, user roles and constraints.

Writing requirements that can be tested

  • One requirement per line, with an ID like FR-1 so tests and tickets can refer to it.
  • Use 'The system shall' and a measurable result, for example 'The system shall send a password reset email within 60 seconds'.
  • Give every requirement a priority such as Must, Should or Could.
  • Write non-functional requirements with numbers: response time, availability, number of users, data retention.
  • Put what is out of scope in writing. It prevents most scope arguments later.

A short example

FR-3: The system shall let an admin export all invoices of a month as CSV. Priority: Must. NFR-2: The export shall finish within 10 seconds for 10,000 invoices. Both lines can be checked by a tester without asking the author what was meant.

FAQ

What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?

Functional requirements say what the system does, such as exporting a report. Non-functional requirements say how well it does it, such as speed, security or availability.

Who writes the SRS?

Usually a product owner or business analyst, with input from the client and the development team. Developers should review it before work starts.

Is an SRS still useful in agile projects?

A lighter version is. Many teams keep a short SRS for scope, roles and non-functional requirements and manage features as user stories.
